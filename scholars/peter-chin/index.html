What happened

The first AHEAD Global Conversation

The first AHEAD Global Conversation took place online on Monday 9 November 2026. Prof Peter Chin of the Faculty of Education, Queen's University, Canada, spoke on Preparing teachers for every community, and colleagues registered from six countries for the inaugural session of AHEAD Global Scholars.

He began with his work at Queen's, where he led programmes that opened the teaching profession to Indigenous, French-language and technological teacher candidates across Ontario. His argument was a simple one: learners need to see themselves in the people who teach them, and a teaching profession that reflects its communities has to be built on purpose.

He then turned to Africa, where since 2018 he has worked with 1 Million Teachers on teacher education. Its graduates go on to lead and support other teachers, part of the response to a teacher shortage that UNESCO counts in the millions. The aim, he said, is to support what educators in the Global South are already building, not to arrive with answers.

The key insights

Three ideas that stayed with us

01

See yourself in your teacher

Learners need to see themselves in the people who teach them. A profession that reflects its communities has to be built on purpose, from who is recruited to who is supported to finish.

02

Partnership, not prescription

Working with the Global South means strengthening what educators there are already building, and avoiding what Peter called colonialism 2.0.

03

AI shaped by communities

Teachers and communities should shape how AI enters the classroom. Used well, it can help learners see themselves in the curriculum, which depends on multicultural content that is part of the material, not added at the margins.

15 million
more teachers needed in sub-Saharan Africa by 2030.

The question is not only how many, but who teaches, what they teach and whose knowledge counts.

From the conversation

What participants asked

How do barriers for Indigenous teacher candidates in Canada compare with those facing African students?

Peter pointed to shared roots: confidence, community support, and systems that quietly keep people out of teaching.

Why is the teacher not equally respected everywhere?

The value a society places on education differs, and can change within a generation. He told the story of a young man from a Maasai community who sold cattle to pay for university and later used his education to support his family.

How do learners come to see themselves in the curriculum?

Through teacher development and change at system level together. He urged university scholars to take part in curriculum committee work, and called for global citizenship education to reach into school curricula, not stay a university conversation about the Sustainable Development Goals.
