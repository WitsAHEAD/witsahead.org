Preparing teachers for every community

In Johannesburg for three weeks in South Africa, Prof Peter Chin of Queen's University opened AHEAD Global Scholars with a conversation about who becomes a teacher, whom learners see in front of them, and what partnership between Canada and Africa should look like.

What happened

The first AHEAD Global Conversation

The first AHEAD Global Conversation took place on Monday 9 November 2026. Prof Peter Chin, for sixteen years Associate Dean of Teacher Education at Queen's University, Canada, joined from Johannesburg, where he was spending twenty days in South Africa. He chose to be with us that week rather than at the Queen's dinner for his 2026 Distinguished Service Award, only the fourth given to a member of the Faculty of Education in the award's 52 years.

He began with the programmes he built at Queen's: multi-session pathways that prepare teachers in six Indigenous communities across Ontario, some reached only by air or, in winter, by roads across frozen lakes, alongside French-language and technological education programmes. The provincial government has since put about 7.5 million Canadian dollars behind them. The point, he said, is not the money but who becomes a teacher, and whom learners then see in front of them.

He then turned to Africa and his partnership with Hakeem Subair and 1 Million Teachers, now in more than 20 countries, with some 90,000 teachers working through its modules and Black Belt graduates who go on to support others. His team is now surveying and interviewing those graduates to learn what has changed in their teaching. The principle throughout was support, not rescue: Africa solving Africa's questions in education, with partners alongside.

The key insights

Three ideas that stayed with us

01

You can't be what you can't see

When Indigenous learners have no Indigenous teachers, the lesson they learn is that people like them cannot teach. A teaching profession that reflects its communities has to be built on purpose, from who is recruited to who is supported to finish.

02

Support, not saviours

Partners from the North cannot fix education in Africa, and should not try. They can support what African educators are already building. Anything else, Peter warned, is colonialism 2.0.

03

Critical digital literacy

AI still carries a Western, male bias in what it draws on. The answer is not to chase cheating but to teach learners to question it, to ask why they are missing from its answers, and to help shape a third space where every learner sees themselves.

15 million
more teachers needed in sub-Saharan Africa by 2030.

The question is not only how many, but who teaches, what they teach and whose knowledge counts.

From the conversation

What participants asked

What assumptions about access carry over from First Nations programmes to Africa?

Confidence. People told for generations that teaching is not for them give up at the first difficulty, so programmes need 360-degree support. In one fly-in Ontario community, 25 of the 70 teachers now come from the community itself.

Where can AI make the most meaningful contribution in African classrooms?

Through critical digital literacy: teaching learners to question AI rather than simply catching cheats, and using it to help build curriculum in which learners recognise their own cultures.

Why are teachers valued so differently from one country to another?

Societies value education differently, and that can change within a generation. He told of a young man from a Maasai community who sold his cattle to go to university and, with his first salary, bought his family more cattle.

How do African teachers grow so learners see themselves in the curriculum?

Move from sage on the stage to guide on the side, and act beyond the classroom: university scholars belong on the committees that write national curricula, and the starting point is truth.

How do you measure the impact of capacity building?

By asking the teachers. His team is surveying and interviewing 1 Million Teachers Black Belt graduates about confidence, practice and their role in mentoring others.

Where does global citizenship education stand?

Mostly at universities. The Sustainable Development Goals have not yet reached school curricula, and they should.
